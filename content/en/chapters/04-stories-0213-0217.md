# Chapter 4 · Two Paths at the Wall: False Break and Genuine Breakout

Two trading days, two opposite paths. On February 13, price made a **false break**, swept the level, and reclaimed it. On February 17, price made a **genuine breakout**, held above the Gamma Flip, and advanced to the Call Wall.

---

## February 13, 2026 · SPX 0DTE: A Textbook Liquidity Sweep

After the open that day, I confirmed two levels:

- **Put Wall @ 6800**
- **Call Wall @ 6900**

The range was clear: a full 100 points from 6800 to 6900.

All I had to do was wait for the market to show which side it wanted.

### The Open: Price Broke the Put Wall, but I Did Nothing

The market sold off hard through the first half hour. Fear rose.

**9:43, 6795.49.**

That was the candle's **low**. Its lower wick pierced the 6800 Put Wall by just over four points—but the same candle **closed at 6799.62**, less than half a point below the wall.

**Four and a half points down, four tenths short on the way back.** The shape alone was half the answer.

Honestly, I did not act immediately.

**A broken wall, by itself, means nothing.**

What matters is how price returns.

The earlier version of me would have acted the moment the Put Wall broke. I would either chase the short—“The level broke; get in”—or buy the dip—“This is support; it has to bounce.”

Both impulses came from the same flaw: an inability to tolerate doing nothing.

Sitting in cash, waiting, and watching price pierce a key level without acting can feel worse than losing money. But I eventually learned that the market's most expensive action is often the one taken from fear of missing out.

The lower wick did not continue. Selling did not extend. Liquidity was swept and then price **reclaimed the level**.

That was the answer.

Those few points below 6800 were a classic trap: stops were cleared, shorts entered, and price reversed higher. The pattern is called a **liquidity sweep**; see the **Appendix Glossary**. February 13 was a classic example at a **Put Wall**.

**A reclaim of 6800 was one signal that made me consider a long.** It was not a universal answer—only my judgment in that moment.

### A Positive-Gamma Regime: The Greatest Test of Patience

After price reclaimed 6800, the path was dull—but the direction was right.

Live platform data placed the session in a **positive-Gamma** regime. Standing above 6800 does not automatically make Gamma positive; see “Gamma Flip” in Chapter 2. Price ground slowly higher while volatility remained suppressed. Keep two things separate: **positive Gamma explains the damping and slower rhythm created by counter-trend hedging; it does not supply the upward direction.** The rise still required net buying, price action, and the reclaimed key level. This kind of market **tests patience more than anything else**.

I used to make my worst mistakes in such “boring” sessions. Price felt too slow, so I traded in and out until fees and slippage consumed the edge.

I thought trading required “action.” Holding still while price ground forward felt like wasted time.

Eventually I learned that not trading is part of trading. A positive-Gamma day is naturally slow. Trying to impose a fast rhythm means fighting the market; the market is not fighting you.

Now I remind myself: **on a positive-Gamma day, slow is fast.**

**But a buyer has to discount that sentence.** The index can afford to grind; the 0DTE Call in your hand cannot. Every additional hour shaves another layer off its time value, and the shaving gets more severe through the afternoon. A positive-Gamma day is friendly to **direction** and hostile to **a buyer's clock**—the index can grind 20 points higher without the premium gaining the equivalent, because Theta is subtracting the whole time.

So the complete version is: **on a positive-Gamma day, trade slowly but do not linger.** What should be slow is how often you act, not how long you hold.

As price ground up toward 6880 in the afternoon, signs to take profit kept appearing.

The 6900 Call Wall was 20 points away. Mechanically, this is why the last stretch gets harder: as price approaches 6900, the Gamma on that stack of Calls **grows**, so a market maker staying neutral has to **sell more into every advance**. The selling in front of a wall is not a psychological level—it is the hedging flow itself thickening.

There is a second layer for a buyer. The closer your Call gets to the wall, the closer it is to being in the money, the more its Delta walks toward 1—and **its own Gamma shrinks**. The acceleration that further upside gives you is decaying, while Theta has not slowed for a single minute: each additional point costs more and pays more linearly.

**Institutional money does not wait for impact.**

Declining Volume near 6880 was one reason I chose to exit.

**The back half of that session verified the decision for me.** After topping around 6880, the index never reached 6900. It slid back all afternoon, fell to about 6820 into the close, and finished at 6836—three points above the prior close, up 0.05% on the day.

I exited at 6880, not far from the session high. Had I held another two hours chasing “one more touch of 6900,” that 0DTE Call would have given back most of the gain under **falling price and time decay together**.

One more note: that late-session slide of several dozen points is a reminder not to hear “positive Gamma” as “insurance.” Positive Gamma compresses **how much price moves per unit of time**. It does not forbid price from falling. A day can net out nearly flat and still contain one continuous slide.

After the close, I left myself three questions. Near the 6800 Put Wall, did I chase the short? Near the 6900 Call Wall, did I chase higher? When I exited, had the signal actually appeared, or had I simply panicked?

Here, the Put Wall was more than support. It was a dividing line between the two sides. A break tested conviction; holding the wall established the directional case.

But the deeper lesson was this: **a liquidity sweep does not succeed every time.** A bounce from a wall can fail. Price can break, refuse to reclaim, and continue lower. This February 13 trade worked because three conditions aligned: selling did not continue after the break and price reclaimed quickly; positive Gamma helped dampen the move; and I did not chase a short at 6795.

The earlier version of me would have shorted the break of 6800, been swept, held the loss, and eventually blown up. Now, after a break, I first watch how price attempts to return. Only after confirmation do I consider direction. The difference was not the GEX chart. It was **execution**.

## February 17, 2026 · SPX 0DTE: From Gamma Flip to Call Wall

The market escaped a negative-Gamma trap, crossed the Gamma Flip, and finally met the Call Wall.

February 17 remains one of the most structurally complete sessions in my memory. Every step from entry to exit could be compared with the GEX map.

---

### Three Coordinates Before the Open

The premarket GEX map marked three key levels:

| Level | Price | Role |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Put Wall | 6800 | Floor below; short-side battle zone |
| Gamma Flip | 6810 | Regime boundary |
| Call Wall | 6850 | Ceiling above; positive-Gamma resistance |

**Range:** 6800 to 6850, or 50 points.

My plan was to let the market show which part of the range it wanted to inhabit.

### The Temptation on the Left and Confirmation on the Right

Price sold off within the first hour. It lost 6810, then lost 6800, and bottomed **below 6775**—more than 25 points under the Put Wall—then ground around down there for nearly an hour before climbing back.

There is a distinction worth making explicit here: **the regime switch is 6810, not 6800.** The moment price broke the 6810 Gamma Flip, market makers' net Gamma turned negative and hedging flipped from “sell rallies, buy dips” to “buy rallies, sell dips”; volatility began to be amplified. 6800 is simply where the Put stack is thickest—that is **thickness**, not a switch. Breaking 6810, then 6800, and still travelling another 25 points lower is exactly what negative Gamma looks like: **a wall can stop contracts; it cannot stop acceleration.**

Catching the falling knife there **felt extremely dangerous**: the further price fell, the more market makers had to sell, which can compound into a downward Gamma squeeze.

**The earlier me:** price breaks 6800 → chase the short.

**The current me:** price breaks 6800 → wait for it to return → watch how it returns.

At 10:59 price regained 6800, then pushed straight through 6810, the Gamma Flip. Two things happened together: reclaiming 6800 showed that bids had returned below, and holding 6810 meant a switch back from negative to positive Gamma—**the market's character had changed.**

One difference is worth pausing on. February 13 was a **sweep**: a four-and-a-half-point wick, reclaimed within minutes, a textbook **liquidity sweep** (see the **Appendix Glossary**). February 17 was not a sweep. Price **genuinely fell in**: 25 points, for nearly an hour. So I do not call that morning a false break.

The signal to wait for is therefore different in each case:

- On February 13 the signal was **reclaiming the Put Wall**—price never truly broke down, so a reclaim was enough to disprove the break.
- On February 17 the signal was **standing above the Gamma Flip**—price had genuinely broken down, and crawling back to 6800 only showed that the decline had stopped. It said nothing about the regime. Whether the regime had changed was a question for 6810.

That is the dividing line behind this chapter's title: **one wall was never truly broken and reclaimed itself; the other was genuinely broken, and only standing back above the switch turned the page.**

### Advancing Under Positive-Gamma Damping—and Meeting the Wall

Above the Gamma Flip, when the platform shows positive Gamma under the common assumption that market makers are net long Gamma, hedging is usually **sell futures as price rises and buy futures as price falls**. That is a counter-trend damping force, not fuel for a rally.

The move from 6810 to 6850 therefore should not be attributed to “positive Gamma pushing price higher.” A more accurate reading is that net buying and trend momentum continued to lift price, while positive-Gamma hedging suppressed volatility, making the advance slower and more prone to pullbacks. I considered the structure relatively clear—but clarity is not a guarantee. It was the combination of reclaimed levels, price action, and regime.

Just after 11:00, price touched **6849.56** and stalled—four tenths of a point short of the 6850 Call Wall.

**Do not buy a Call at the Call Wall.** That was my reminder to myself that day. At the foot of the wall, every further step up must first absorb the market makers' thickening hedge offers, and momentum drains. Meanwhile the Call in your hand is close to being in the money, its own Gamma is shrinking, the payoff is turning linear, and Theta has not slowed at all. **I began considering an exit around there**—not waiting for the impact itself, but leaving in front of the wall.

**What happened next came in two stages, and both are worth recording.**

**Stage one: the wall won.** After touching it, price could not hold. It slid all the way back to around 6810, the foot of the Gamma Flip—a full 40 points. Anyone who left early gave up none of that.

**Stage two: the wall lost.** A second push came in the afternoon, and around 13:40 price genuinely broke 6850, reaching a high near 6868 and spending the afternoon above the wall. Into the close it was pulled back again, finishing at 6843—below the wall once more.

Put the two stages together and you have the wall's full character that day: **it held once, it was broken once, and it still closed price underneath itself.** I left in front of it, so I did not capture those dozen-odd points above. But capturing them required first sitting through the 40-point retracement in between—and on 0DTE, sitting through a retracement costs more than price. It costs those two hours of time value.

That is why I would rather leave in front of the wall: **a wall is not unbreakable. It usually grinds you down before it breaks.**

| Stage | Logic |
| --- | --- |
| Entry | Technical acceleration + break above Gamma Flip = my trend-confirmation signal at the time |
| Hold | Trend remained intact while 6810 held underfoot; positive Gamma explained the slower rhythm, not the upward direction |
| Invalidation | Back below 6810 = the regime flips to negative Gamma and the reason to hold is gone |
| Exit | Approaching the Call Wall = thickening hedge offers plus the buyer's decaying Gamma; leave in front of the wall, do not wait for impact |

---

These two days were very similar—and entirely different.

They are alike because both began with a broken wall: February 13 fell below the Put Wall, and so did February 17.

They are unlike because the depth of the break was not remotely comparable. One stabbed four and a half points down and reclaimed within minutes; the other fell 25 points and ground there for nearly an hour. The first called for waiting on **a reclaim of the wall**; the second called for waiting on **standing back above the switch**. The same phrase—“broke the Put Wall”—points at completely different things afterward.

What I did, though, was the same in both cases: **do nothing at the instant of the break. Let the wall prove whether the move is false or real.**

On February 13, price reclaimed the wall, so I recognized a false break. On February 17, price held above the Gamma Flip, so I recognized a genuine breakout. Neither trade came from “guessing the direction correctly.” It came from **waiting until the structure spoke before deciding whether to act.**

That is harder to learn than any line. Waiting begins with an admission: **I do not know what happens next.** I can wait until probability tilts toward me and the structure supports the trade, then rely on discipline. Even if it means giving up the last stretch in front of the Call Wall, I do not stand at the wall trading against the market makers' hedge.

Anyone can guess direction. The difficult work lies in two actions that resist instinct: **hold your hand when the wall breaks; withdraw before impact.** Those two actions made February 13 and February 17 feel so clean.

I traded both days well. Profit was not what made them memorable.

I remember holding my hand twice.

The first time, the Put Wall broke. I neither chased the short nor bought the dip. I waited for price to finish the move and return on its own.

The second time, price climbed toward the Call Wall. I did not wait for “just a little more.” I left before the wall.

That wall on February 17 was in fact broken later in the day—but that is something I only knew afterward. **What was visible at the time was only the hedge offers thickening in front of it.** Betting on what is visible now, rather than judging yourself against a result you learn later, took me a long time to practice.

Technique did not teach me those actions. They were carved into muscle memory by every earlier time I failed to hold my hand.

Profit gets spent. Charts turn to the next page. But the two memories remain: **hold your hand at the break; withdraw before the wall.**

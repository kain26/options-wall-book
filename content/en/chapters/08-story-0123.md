On January 23, 2026, SPX closed at 6915.61, with an intraday low of 6895.50 and a high of 6932.89—a 0.54% range for the session. Thirty-seven points, in an entire day.

Price action was as bland as lukewarm water. Underneath, the structure was dense: **stop run, reversal from a GEX extreme, and a close drawn toward the Gamma Flip**—three linked stages.

And those three stages were not spread evenly across the day: **the most violent of them happened in the twenty minutes after the open.**

---

## Why 6895 Instead of 6900?

The market tested the low three times and reached 6895.50. Not 6900, not 6898—6895.

Spread the day's intraday chart out (**Figure 8-1**) and all three tests sit inside the same twenty minutes, **09:30 to 09:50**. Each one stopped a few points under 6900, and none of them managed to drive the round number meaningfully deeper.

The earlier version of me would think: 6900 is a major round number and therefore strong support. I would place a buy order at 6900, a stop at 6898 or 6899, and feel safe as long as the number held.

I eventually understood something else.

Large numbers of **sell stops** often sit just below 6900. Once triggered, they become market sell orders and supply liquidity. Institutional participants sometimes do not need to guess direction. They can push price through a round number, activate stops, and take the other side of fearful selling several points lower.

That is a **stop run** (**Appendix Glossary**).

In hindsight—not as an intraday certainty—the three tests looked like this:

| Test | Time | Low | My interpretation |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- |
| First | 09:33 | 6900.88 | A probe right against the round number, no break—checking whether there is anything down there |
| Second | 09:35–09:38 | 6895.50 | **Sliced 4.5 points straight through**, triggering stops below the number in a block |
| Third | 09:45–09:49 | 6896.9 | Back to 6905, then one more press down to confirm nobody is left below |

Note that the **second** test, not the first, made the low of the day. **The sequence is itself part of the play:** probe along the line, cut through once to flush the stops out, then press back down once to confirm the space below is empty. Only after all three were done did the tape get light.

After 09:50 price never traded below 6900 again—**in the remaining six hours of the session, that line was not touched once.**

**So what confirms intraday that the sweep is finished?** What I used that day is the same old line from Chapter 5: **VWAP.**

At the moment of the 09:36 low, price was just over 6896 and the average price was around 6903—**seven points under the cost line.** After the third press down, price **reclaimed VWAP at roughly 09:52** and did not look back: 6913 by 09:59, 6916 by 10:05.

**Stops being swept means “this may be a low”; reclaiming VWAP is what means “I may act.”** The first is other people handing over their positions. The second is your own reason coming into existence.

The most important change in my process was to stop placing stops directly at round numbers. That sounds easy and is difficult.

“Round numbers are support” was not merely learned; it had grown into my way of seeing charts. Removing it required more than moving a stop. It meant fighting years of habit.

After January 23, I stopped placing the order at 6900 and watched structure below 6890 instead. The first weeks felt deeply uncomfortable. I kept thinking, “What if price really does bounce at 6900?” Eventually I accepted that missing one possible rebound is much easier than being stopped at the low and watching price return without me.

The real sweep level is usually those few points just under the round number. What is interesting is that the two days measured almost exactly the same depth: **January 23 was 4.5 points below 6900 (6895.50); February 13 was 4.5 points below 6800 (6795.49).**

Similar depth does not mean the script transfers. The **character of the two battlefields was completely different**—one a purely psychological round number, the other a Put Wall you can actually measure on the GEX map. The last section of this chapter opens that up.

> *A round number is a psychological level, not necessarily a structural one. The liquidity hunt targets your stop, not your directional opinion.*

## The 13:45 GEX Extreme: What I Watch When the Data Looks Worst

The day's classic moment appeared around 13:45.

**Volume GEX** reached approximately **−1000B**—an extreme negative-Gamma reading (the B is my own platform's scale; do not compare the absolute number against another vendor's, as Chapter 2 explains). On the surface, this was the most frightening condition: market makers were extremely short Gamma and volatility should theoretically accelerate downward.

But price **did not continue falling sharply**.

In hindsight, an intraday negative-Gamma extreme can mean the short-side hedging trade has become deeply crowded. Much of the required selling may already have occurred. If price stops making new lows, I become alert: this may be **seller exhaustion**, not the beginning of another collapse.

From **13:45 to 14:10**, Volume GEX converged away from the extreme and finally **flipped positive**—the blue box on Figure 8-1 is where I marked “NET GEX back positive” on the tape at the time. Hedging pressure came off, shorts covered, and over that same stretch price pushed from around 6904 up to 6922.

**These are two faces of one event:** GEX turning positive and price reclaiming 6915 happened inside the same window. The next section is about that 6915 line.

**An extreme does not guarantee a reversal.** March 26 showed that an extreme can become more extreme. But on January 23, **extreme GEX + price refusing to follow lower** was a combination worth noticing.

GEX extremes are an **emotional thermometer**, not a directional command.

> *An extreme says the trade is crowded. Crowding can intensify. Price must stop following before the message becomes actionable.*

## Gamma Flip 6915: Why Did Price Close There?

That afternoon, the Gamma Flip hovered near 6915–6916.

The close was **6915.61**.

After clearing liquidity at 6895, price climbed gradually and stopped near 6915. Why there?

Avoid mystical talk of “structural gravity” and return to the **Pin** from Chapter 3. Near the close, when large open interest concentrates at a strike K, the Gamma of that strike becomes enormous. Once price runs above K, the market maker's Delta adjustment sells it back down; once price runs below K, the same adjustment lifts it back up—back and forth, until price behaves as though it were glued near K.

**But there is a precondition here that cannot be skipped.** That pull-back-toward-the-strike hedging is **positive-Gamma** behavior. The market maker has to be net long Gamma at that strike in order to sell rallies and buy dips (Chapter 2); if it is negative Gamma, the very same strike only pushes price further away. So a Pin is not “thick OI therefore pinned.” It is “thick OI **and** a counter-trend hedging direction—that is what pins.”

That day, **6915 sat right on top of the heaviest cluster of OI in the session** (running up toward 6920, where OI stacked highest). What made it neater still is that this heaviest strike also landed on the **Gamma Flip** line—the boundary between positive and negative GEX, the balance point between the two sides.

Two forces pointed toward the same region: **Pin geometry**, where heavy OI attracted price near expiration, and **Gamma Flip structure**, where a return to the sign-change boundary suppressed volatility.

**How I read the Gamma Flip:**

- Holding above the Flip → positive-Gamma region → lower volatility and greater pinning
- Falling below the Flip → negative-Gamma region → amplified volatility

During the final segment, the market shifted from negative-Gamma agitation back into positive-Gamma stability. That is why a 0DTE Put held through the last hour could lose time value and volatility premium extremely quickly. The candles were not lying; **the market's character had changed.**

> *The 6915 close was neither pure coincidence nor proof of manipulation. The heaviest OI and the Gamma Flip overlapped, quietly shifting probability toward the same region. I could not predict the exact closing cent, but I knew the day's attraction sat near 6915.*

## Three Battlefields on One Chart

**Figure 8-2** divides the day vertically into three zones: the 6895 meat grinder, negative-Gamma disorder between 6900 and 6915, and positive-Gamma stability above 6915. The psychological level was 6900; the sweep occurred at 6895.

**Before reading that figure, be clear about its shelf life.** These three zones are a map of **the last stretch before the close**, not a ruler valid for the whole day: the 6915 Gamma Flip only settled there in the afternoon, and NET GEX did not flip positive until around 14:10. Before that, price ran perfectly wild above 6915—between 10:00 and 14:10 it swung **thirty-one points, from 6902 to 6933**.

**The same line is nothing at all while GEX has not turned positive, and only starts pinning people once it has.** This is what Chapter 2 means by “the map changes”: the level is fixed, the character is alive.

On January 23, I used **Figure 8-2** to work out which zone I was in:

**6895–6900: the meat grinder**

- Liquidity-hunt zone
- Stops trigger, panic selling appears, and larger buyers absorb
- Do not place the stop here and do not chase a short here

**6900–6915: negative-Gamma disorder**

- High volatility and pro-cyclical market-maker hedging
- Negative GEX amplifies movement
- If trading, enter and exit quickly. The profitable move that day occurred in this zone

**Above 6915: positive-Gamma stability** (only genuinely in force after NET GEX turned positive at 14:10)

- Volatility dries up and price becomes pinned
- Small rises, small pullbacks, and a slow grind
- Think range or cash. For a long-premium buyer like me, the game was largely over

That last zone is not something I wrote down by feel; it was measured: **in the four hours before 14:10 the range was thirty-one points; in the ninety minutes from 14:10 to the close, only fifteen.** Same index, same 6915—half its character replaced.

> *First identify the battlefield. Only then choose how to fight.*

It took years to believe that sentence.

I once asked only, “Up or down?” Then I filled the screen with evidence for my answer.

Now I first ask, “Which zone am I in?” A meat grinder, disorderly region, and stable region require different approaches. The same price carries different meaning in different battlefields.

You do not need to win everywhere. You need to recognize, “This is not my battlefield,” and stay away.

## January 23 vs February 13: Two Liquidity Sweeps

February 13 in Chapter 4 and January 23 here both look on the surface like “break → sweep the stops → reclaim,” and even the depth is nearly identical (4.5 points in both cases), but they are read differently.

**They do not even have the same name.** The umbrella term for a sweep is a **liquidity sweep**; the variety that specifically goes after a round number is a **stop run** (**Appendix Glossary**). February 13 was the former—what broke was a Put Wall on the GEX map. January 23 was the latter—what broke was a psychological round number.

**Figure 8-3** puts the two days side by side: the 6900 line on the left is a phantom, with no wall on the GEX map at all; the 6800 line on the right is real, a structure you can measure on the map. The sweep depth is identical; what sits underneath is nothing alike.

While we are here, one impression that is easy to form from the words alone needs correcting: **“positive-Gamma slow grind” does not mean “barely moved.”** On February 13 price ground from 6795 all the way up to 6876—a full **85 points**; whereas January 23, the “high-volatility negative-Gamma” day, had a total range of only **37 points**, swung back and forth twice. **Positive Gamma suppresses volatility per unit of time, not direction** (Chapter 2)—and nothing shows this as clearly as the two lines of Figure 8-3 side by side.

January 23 left me with **round numbers + stop runs**. February 13 left me with **structural walls + positive-Gamma patience**.

As for how much “does it hold after the reclaim” matters, March 26 in Chapter 6 already played out the counter-example: price reclaimed there too, and held for twenty minutes.

Put the two days together: **a liquidity sweep can look the same while the battlefield's character differs—it cannot be copied and pasted.**

> *The same pattern can produce an entirely different outcome in a different Gamma regime.*

---

January 23 did not teach that tomorrow must sweep the same level. It taught: **identify the battlefield before discussing direction.** Below a round number, beside an extreme, and near the Flip, different rules apply. Forcing the February 13 wall-reclaim script onto 6895—or forcing the January 23 stop-run logic onto every Put Wall—confuses battlefields.

As these seven trading-day reviews close, I hope one habit remains: after a gain or loss, name **where the trade failed or succeeded**, rather than saying only that the market was strange. Structural language gives the experience a name. Turning that name into discipline is another task.

The first eight chapters mostly taught how to see the market. The next changes perspective: think like a market maker.
